## What's Wrong with Standard Economic Models?

Mainstream macroeconomic models — Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE) models in particular — have long treated the natural environment as an afterthought, if they acknowledge it at all. The economy is modeled as a closed circular flow: households supply labour and capital, firms produce output, and the system converges toward some equilibrium. Nature appears only as an exogenous shock — a hurricane here, a drought there.

This framing has three critical blind spots. First, it ignores **thermodynamic constraints**: the economy is not a closed loop but an open system that extracts low-entropy energy and matter from the biosphere and returns high-entropy waste. Second, it misrepresents **how money and credit work**: banks create money endogenously, and finance actively shapes investment patterns — including whether or not green investment materialises. Third, it assumes **equilibrium**: the economy is always self-correcting back toward a stable steady state, which makes structural crises and regime shifts nearly invisible by design.

Ecological Stock-Flow Consistent (E-SFC) models were developed precisely to overcome these limitations.

## The SFC Foundation: Accounting All the Way Down

Before we add ecology, we need to understand the underlying SFC framework. Stock-Flow Consistent models, systematised by **Wynne Godley** and **Marc Lavoie** in their landmark 2007 book *Monetary Economics*, are built on one uncompromising principle: **every financial flow must come from somewhere and go somewhere**.

In practice, this means every model must pass two accounting tests:

1. **The flow consistency condition**: the sum of all sector income and expenditure flows across the economy must be zero. One sector's deficit is another's surplus — always.
2. **The stock consistency condition**: the change in any sector's stock of assets or liabilities across a period must equal the net flows during that period.

These constraints are captured in a **Balance Sheet Matrix** (tracking who owns what: equities, loans, deposits, bonds) and a **Transactions Flow Matrix** (tracking who pays whom: wages, taxes, investment, interest). Together, they enforce a discipline that DSGE models routinely violate — where money can seemingly appear from nowhere to clear markets.

The SFC framework is non-equilibrium by nature. It models discrete time periods and tracks the dynamic evolution of stocks and flows rather than assuming a long-run equilibrium toward which everything gravitates. This makes it well-suited for studying financial fragility, debt dynamics, and demand-driven growth — hallmarks of the **post-Keynesian** tradition.

## Bridging to the Biosphere: The Ecological Extension

Standard SFC models, as developed by Godley, Lavoie, and others, were purely monetary. Nature did not appear. The ecological extension emerged in the 2010s as a response to growing evidence that macrofinancial dynamics and environmental crises are deeply entangled.

The key innovation is integrating a **physical accounting layer** alongside the monetary one. This draws on **Nicolas Georgescu-Roegen**'s flow-fund model, which distinguishes between:

- **Stocks**: accumulated inventories that can be drawn down (fossil fuels, minerals, ecosystem capital)
- **Flows**: throughputs of energy and material that pass through the production process (energy use, material extraction, waste generation)
- **Funds**: productive capacities that are not consumed but may depreciate (capital equipment, labour, ecosystem services)

In an E-SFC model, the economy produces output by combining monetary inputs (wages, credit) with physical inputs (energy, materials). The production process transforms low-entropy inputs into high-entropy outputs and waste. Emissions accumulate in the atmosphere, raising temperatures. Climate damages feed back into economic productivity, capital depreciation, and financial stability.

The critical point: **money and matter must be tracked simultaneously**. A green investment subsidy looks different when you account not just for its financial multiplier effect but also for its energy and material throughput implications.

## The DEFINE Framework: A Landmark Contribution

The most developed E-SFC framework is **DEFINE** — Dynamic Ecosystem-FINance-Economy — developed by **Yannis Dafermos**, **Maria Nikolaidi**, and **Giorgos Galanis**, primarily at SOAS University of London.

First published in a foundational 2017 paper in *Ecological Economics*, DEFINE has since expanded into a family of models:

- **DEFINE-GLOBAL**: A large-scale model of the global economy and ecosystem, capable of generating long-run climate-macrofinancial scenarios.
- **DEFINE-UK**: A country-specific model calibrated to the British economy.
- **Simplified DEFINE variants**: Smaller, pedagogically-oriented models that isolate specific mechanisms — useful for teaching and policy communication.

DEFINE explicitly models:

| Layer | Key variables |
|---|---|
| **Macroeconomy** | Output, investment, consumption, wages, profits, employment |
| **Financial system** | Bank lending, firm debt, household wealth, central bank policy |
| **Energy & materials** | Energy use, material throughput, waste generation, recycling |
| **Climate** | CO₂ emissions, temperature change, tipping points |
| **Ecosystem** | Ecosystem capital, biodiversity proxies, resource depletion |

A defining feature of DEFINE is its treatment of **money endogeneity**: commercial banks create credit in response to loan demand, and the availability and cost of finance directly shapes whether green investment materialises. This is fundamentally different from loanable funds thinking, where savings must precede investment.

## What Can E-SFC Models Tell Us?

### 1. Climate–Finance Feedback Loops

One of the most important contributions of E-SFC models is revealing how climate change can destabilise the financial system — and vice versa. Dafermos, Nikolaidi, and Galanis (2018) introduced the concept of a **"climate Minsky moment"**: a sudden reassessment of climate-related financial risks that triggers a cascade of asset write-downs, credit contraction, and macroeconomic instability.

The mechanism works as follows. Climate change increases physical capital depreciation (infrastructure damaged by floods, heat, storms). Firms' balance sheets deteriorate. Banks tighten credit conditions. Investment collapses. Unemployment rises. The resulting recession reduces emissions — but through economic contraction, not deliberate transition.

This feedback loop is invisible in models that treat the financial system as a veil over real economic activity.

### 2. Comparing Green Policy Instruments

E-SFC models have been used to rigorously compare three types of green fiscal policy:

- **Carbon taxes**: raise the price of fossil fuels, incentivising substitution. In SFC models, the revenue recycling mechanism matters enormously — returning revenue as lump-sum transfers has very different distributional and demand effects than using it for public green investment.
- **Green subsidies**: lower the cost of renewable investment. Effective for stimulating private green capital formation, but may be regressive if firms capture rents.
- **Green public investment**: directly expands the green capital stock. In post-Keynesian models, the demand multiplier effect is significant, especially during periods of private-sector deleveraging.

The key finding from Dafermos and Nikolaidi's 2019 comparative analysis: **green public investment consistently outperforms carbon taxes and subsidies** on both macroeconomic and ecological dimensions, particularly when the economy is below full capacity. Carbon taxes alone are insufficient and risk being contractionary if not carefully designed.

### 3. Degrowth Scenarios

E-SFC models are well-suited to evaluating **degrowth** — the deliberate downscaling of economic throughput to remain within planetary boundaries. A recurring concern about degrowth proposals is macrofinancial: if output contracts, do debt-deflation spirals emerge? Does unemployment surge?

E-SFC models can trace these dynamics explicitly. Results are sensitive to institutional design. A degrowth scenario with:
- Reduced working hours (distributing work across a larger workforce)
- Strong social spending
- Debt restructuring for households and firms

...can avoid the debt spiral and maintain employment, though it requires active fiscal and monetary policy coordination. By contrast, degrowth imposed through austerity — reducing public spending and letting market forces adjust — produces precisely the crisis dynamics critics fear.

### 4. Central Bank Green Mandates

A growing application area is **green monetary policy**. E-SFC models allow researchers to model the effects of:
- Differential reserve requirements that penalise fossil fuel lending
- Green asset purchase programmes by central banks
- Mandatory climate stress testing that affects bank lending capacity

These tools operate through the credit channel: by altering the cost and availability of finance for brown vs. green activities, central banks can influence the composition of investment even without changing overall demand.

## E-SFC vs. Other Approaches

It is worth situating E-SFC models within the broader landscape of ecological-economic modelling:

| Approach | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs)** | Global reach, detailed energy/climate modules | DSGE foundations, optimising representative agent, weak on finance and distribution |
| **Agent-Based Models (ABMs)** | Heterogeneous agents, emergent behaviour | Difficult to calibrate, less tractable analytically |
| **Input-Output Models** | Detailed sectoral structure | Static, no dynamics, no financial sector |
| **E-SFC Models** | Financial realism, demand dynamics, thermodynamic accounting | Aggregate sectors, calibration challenges, less detailed energy modules |

No single framework dominates. In practice, E-SFC models are increasingly being combined with Input-Output tables (for sectoral detail) and Agent-Based approaches (for distributional heterogeneity).

## Limitations and Open Frontiers

E-SFC models represent a significant advance, but several limitations remain:

**Aggregate structure**: Most E-SFC models treat the economy in a small number of aggregate sectors (households, firms, banks, government, central bank). The rich sectoral detail of Input-Output analysis is largely absent.

**Beyond carbon**: Most E-SFC models focus on CO₂ emissions and temperature. Other planetary boundaries — biodiversity loss, water scarcity, nitrogen cycles, ocean acidification — are only beginning to be incorporated.

**Calibration**: Matching E-SFC models to empirical data is non-trivial. Parameters governing, say, the ecological damage function or the sensitivity of bank lending to climate risk are not directly observable.

**Expectations and behaviour**: E-SFC models typically use simple behavioural rules for investment, saving, and pricing. Richer microfoundations for how firms and households respond to ecological crises are an active research frontier.

Despite these limitations, E-SFC models have rapidly established themselves as a leading tool for heterodox ecological macroeconomics — and are increasingly attracting interest from central banks and finance ministries seeking alternatives to DSGE orthodoxy.

---

## Why It Matters

The case for E-SFC models is ultimately political-economic. The models you use shape the policies you recommend. DSGE-based analyses tend to favour carbon pricing as a market correction to an externality — the solution is to get the price right and let markets adjust. E-SFC analyses reveal a more complex picture: financial instability, demand dynamics, distributional conflict, and thermodynamic constraints all matter, and they interact in ways that market price signals alone cannot resolve.

In a moment of accelerating ecological crisis and growing macrofinancial fragility, models that take both seriously are not a luxury. They are a necessity.
